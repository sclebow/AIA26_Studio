# Sensi / Final review · spoken script

The arc reads WHAT then WHY then the three acts. WHAT is the cover plus the sensory layer. WHY is the research gap, the ripple, and the same plan read through two people. Then the three acts, each opened on its own slide and interleaved with a recorded clip: Act 1 Onboard, then It's Personal and the design levers and the coupling and veto, then Act 2 Shape with the scoring engine, the room graph, and the checkpoint, then Act 3 Report and the rendered output, then a combined honest close. Real app screenshots appear only at the proof moments (the CAD plan and scores on layout 202, the persona card, the before and after render). Every conceptual visual is a built vector artifact.

**Presenters.** Maria 1 to 7 (cover through Act 1) · Charles 9 to 16 (the persona, the how, and Act 2) · Lakzhmy 17 to 21 (Act 3 through the close). **Emilie** records the 3 demo voiceovers (in `demo-scripts-week09.md`). Handoffs: Maria to Charles is masked by Clip 1; Charles spans Clip 2; Charles to Lakzhmy is a clean break at Act 3.

**1 · Cover · Maria · ~19s**

Hi, we're Team 02, and this is Sensi. It reads a floor plan and tells you how each room will feel, across six senses, for the one person who will live in it. Why would you want that? Let me show you what it is, and why it matters.

**2 · What is Sensi · Maria · ~34s**

So, what is Sensi? In the AEC we model a building in layers: structure, cost, energy, code. Every one of them has a number. The way a space will feel to be in never did. Sensi adds that layer. It reads the plan and scores how each room feels across six senses, the sensory layer on top of the stack. Three things make it ours. It is personal, scored for one person, not an average. It is honest, we never average the six, the worst sense carries the weight. And the senses are coupled, they pull on each other.

**3 · The gap · Maria · ~32s**

We did not start from the product, we started from the research. In the AEC we measure energy, cost, and code, but never how a room feels. And comfort itself is usually studied one sense at a time, in isolation, on the left. The research says otherwise. Spence and colleagues, in 2020, the multisensory mind: you take a room in through all your senses at once. And a 2025 cross-modal review: the senses are coupled, one moderates another. So the honest picture is on the right, one coupled system, where changing one sense moves the others, and some links run only one way.

**4 · The ripple · Maria · ~30s**

Take one ordinary move, a bigger window. The daylight gets better, that is the upgrade you see. But the same glass is a thinner sound barrier, so noise gets in, and it leaks heat, so the room is colder in winter. Those are the costs you do not see. And here is the ripple: the noise it let in even dims the daylight you gained, one sense moderating another. One move, and the whole room shifts. So we asked, could a designer catch this while it is still a plan?

**5 · The same plan, two people · Maria · ~24s**

Here is why we start with you. This is one plan, read through two people. Slide the lens to a child, who minds noise, and the loud living room lights up as the problem. Slide it to a grandmother, who minds warmth, and the cold bedroom does instead. The plan never changes; who is standing in it decides what you feel. That is why Sensi opens by learning who you are.

**6 · The three acts · Maria · ~28s**

So here is the whole shape of it. Sensi is a conversation in three acts. In Onboard, you tell it who you are, and it builds your sensory persona. In Shape, you work the plan in plain language, and it scores, edits, and shows the ripple. In Report, your scores become a first-person image of each room. You talk, it answers, and the output of each act carries into the next. Let's zoom into the first.

**7 · Act 1 / Onboard · Maria · ~28s · then hand to Clip 1**

Act one is Onboard, the first act of the conversation. You answer a few questions, about the senses that matter to you and who else lives here. Then you teach it your eye, with a curated moodboard. It folds all of that into one thing: your sensory persona, the lens for everything that follows. This is easier to watch than to describe, so let's watch.

**9 · It's personal · Charles · ~30s · opens after Clip 1**

Three inputs compile into one set of weights, one per sense. Your words from the quiz: name a sense, like noise, and it climbs. Your eye from the moodboard: warm wood lifts touch, plants lift smell. And your household: a child, a grandmother, a cat each lean the scores toward whoever feels a problem most. A higher weight raises that sense's alert bar, so Sensi flags it sooner for you. The formula is in the footnote; the concept is what matters. Now we have your persona, and we are ready to shape a plan. That is Act 2.

**10 · The design levers · Charles · ~24s**

So how does Sensi read a room? Comfort is driven by a handful of design moves: which way it faces, how much glass, whether it breathes, how big it is, the surfaces, and what is next door. Each lever pulls on one or more senses, and the colour says which way: green helps, red harms, orange is a trade-off. Glazing helps the visual but harms the acoustic, and cuts both ways on thermal. These levers are the first half of how a score is built.

**11 · Coupling and the veto · Charles · ~28s · the core**

Here is the centre of the project. The levers move each sense, but the senses also move each other. A failing sense drags its neighbours down. My favourite cell: noise harms how bright a room feels, but the mirror is empty, because light does nothing to loudness. The coupling runs one way. Then the honest part: to turn six senses into one number we refuse to average. The worst sense vetoes, half the mean, half the single worst. So a kitchen mostly fine lands at 0.34, floored by smell.

**12 · Act 2 / Shape · Charles · ~32s · then hand to Clip 2**

Now the agent, Act two opened up. On the left, what you say. In the middle, the work it triggers. On the right, Sensi's answer, in plain words. Ask how a room feels, and it scores the room, finds the conflicts, and tells you it is at 0.41. Say change the walls to cork, and it plans the edit, keeps the plan sound, re-scores, and reports the ripple. Or ask to see it a new way, and it draws the galaxy. The other two acts sit collapsed, above and below. Let's watch it happen.

**14 · The scoring engine · Charles · ~30s · after Clip 2**

You watched it score a kitchen; here is the engine underneath, on layout 202. It is deterministic, not a guess: each sense starts at a baseline, the levers move it, the senses couple, and the worst one vetoes the average. The two ringed in red fall hard, sound at 0.28, a hard, boxy room by the living and bedroom, and smell at 0.33, mechanical ventilation and low glazing. Every rule traces to a real standard, the room lands at 0.41, no black box.

**15 · Room to room · Charles · ~28s**

Take the kitchen you just scored, and zoom out to the whole plan. Now the doors light up, each carrying the senses that bleed between rooms: the kitchen's noise and smell reach the bedroom, the busiest room. It is a real graph, rooms as nodes, doors as edges, and it finds the hubs and the bridges. So comfort becomes a zoning problem you can see, straight from the autism sensory-design research.

**16 · Every edit, tracked · Charles · ~22s**

Every edit is kept as a checkpoint, so you watch the plan improve honestly. Each line is a sense, rising as it gets better. A single-room change moves a sense or two; a layout-wide change ripples through everything, and that strong weave is the coupling working, not noise. And it sets up the next act: only committed checkpoints carry forward, so the Report renders the plan you actually kept.

Your persona and the look you curated come back, the cached scores become a prompt, and the prompt becomes an image of how each room actually feels. The honest rule: only the extreme senses speak. Take a look.

**19 · Scores to prompt to image · Lakzhmy · ~28s · after Clip 3**

This is the real screen, and what you saw is generated, not stock photos. On the left, the room's scores write the prompt. The honest rule: only the extreme senses speak. A clearly bad or good sense writes a phrase, in its sense colour, and the comfortable middle stays silent. On the right, the same room before and after an edit: same angle, only the change moving. **20 · What we learned · Lakzhmy · ~40s**

What holds, and what opens. The trade-offs became legible: ripple and veto changed which rooms we worried about. Two build notes: routing only labels the turn in under a second; the felt read takes about twenty-four, because it reasons over all six scores, their couplings, and your persona. And our renders run 2.75 times faster than OpenAI. Then the open ground: could we ever prove it? With no ground-truth data, eighteen of twenty-five couplings surviving a vote is agreement, not truth. Is the AI thinking, or just talking? And does a confident score help a designer decide, or decide for them? The one we leave you with: we made the layer of the senses legible. What else can we make legible?

**21 · Thanks · Lakzhmy · ~12s**

That's Sensi. Comfort, made legible while a building is still a plan. Thank you. We'd love your questions.
